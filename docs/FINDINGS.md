# Findings and benchmarks

What we measured while building the autonomous support agent and its decision layer, and what each result changed in
the design here. Numbers marked *(prior system)* come from our full agent benchmark (750 simulated merchant cases,
LLM planner + gate) and are not re-run by this repo; Jev's numbers are reproduced by `python -m mappa.train_jev`.

## Benchmark setup

- **Simulated world, 750 cases.** 20 in-distribution families over settlements, refunds, Soundbox devices and KYC,
  each encoding a trap a careless agent falls into: a bank that is down (retrying is useless), a settlement already
  credited late (retrying pays twice), an account added 3 hours after a new-device login (likely takeover).
- **Splits.** train 240 · cal 180 (only for thresholds) · test 180 · **ood 90** (6 families never seen in training:
  duplicate UTR, refund to a different UPI ID, stolen device, law-enforcement freeze, open chargeback, telecom outage)
  · **adv 60** (injections in CRM notes, social engineering in the merchant's message).
- **Rupee cost model.** A wrong autonomous action is priced by what it does: money to a wrong or risky account costs
  its full amount, a double refund its amount, a needless device swap ₹1,500, a missed fix ₹200, a harmless reversible
  misstep ₹20. *Costly* ≥ ₹100, *severe* ≥ ₹1,000.
- **Statistics.** Every policy is scored on the same plans, so differences come from the decision rule alone;
  paired McNemar tests and 2,000-sample bootstrap intervals.

## 1. Gate the action, not the text *(prior system)*

| all held-out cases | wrong autonomous action | costly | severe | harm / case |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| LLM agent, no gate | 11.5% | | | ₹64 |
| with the gate (rules + calibrated verifier + conformal λ̂ + expected-loss rule) | **4.8%** [2.7, 7.3] | **0** | **0** | **₹1** |

Every remaining wrong action was a ₹20 reversible reset on an unseen case type. In distribution the gate made no
wrong action (0.0%) at 71% autonomy; on the adversarial split none at all.

**Used here:** hard rules first, a calibrated probability second, conformal risk control for the threshold, and a
rupee ceiling (`AUTO_CEILING_INR`) that keeps large money movements with humans whatever the confidence.

## 2. The guarantee holds; it needs exchangeability *(prior system)*

Over 200 random cal/test re-splits, conformal risk control kept the realised wrong-action rate at a mean of 0.9% for
α = 2%, above α in only 3.5% of splits. Learn-Then-Test (high-probability version) gave the same 4.8% overall at 55%
autonomy. Off-distribution the guarantee does not apply (exchangeability breaks); that gap is covered by hard rules,
the rupee ceiling and per-scenario revocation, which is why all three exist here.

## 3. Verbal confidence is not confidence *(prior system)*

Selective-prediction quality (AURC, lower is better) in distribution: LLM self-reported confidence 0.053, semantic
entropy over re-plans 0.036, our verifier **0.004** (13× better than self-confidence). ECE in distribution: 0.049
self-reported vs 0.005 verifier. **Used here:** Jev is a separate scoring model with fitted temperatures; nothing
routes on what a generator says about itself.

## 4. A stronger planner is not the fix *(prior system)*

With a much cheaper planner, the ungated agent was wrong on 15.2% of cases (7.0% costly); behind the same gate, 5.8%
wrong and **0% costly**. The gate, not the model size, carries the safety.

## 5. Untrusted text: patterns + judge, state checks first *(prior system)*

Held-out attack suite (goal-disjoint from development, 79 attacks / 79 hard-negative benign texts, English, Hinglish,
Devanagari Hindi):

| detector | attacks caught | false flags on benign |
|---|---:|---:|
| **our guard (patterns ∨ LLM judge)** | **100%** [95.4, 100] | 2.5% |
| precise patterns alone | 58% | 0% |
| a popular open prompt-guard model | 15% | 0% |
| a DeBERTa injection classifier | 70% | 2.5% (40% on our calibration benign set) |

On an external, generic injection set the guard caught only 40%. Detection is therefore a second line; the first is
state: what the systems of record show, checked before any write.

**Lessons that shaped `textguard.py`:**
- Bare keywords ("RBI", "urgent", "@ybl") flagged honest merchants. Only patterns that target an attempt to steer the
  agent (instruction overrides, verification waivers, "don't escalate") are kept.
- The imperative-sensitive classifier flagged "Please refund ₹10,770 for transaction T403" as an injection; we rejected it.
- Our first attack suite was generated by a safety-tuned LLM that silently wrote *benign* texts labelled as attacks,
  so a correct detector looked like it was missing attacks. Build attacks by construction (goal + wrapper) and split
  by goal before composing.
- A caller's words can only push a ticket towards a human, never away from one.

## 6. Multilingual encoder, not a bigger LLM *(prior system)*

Intent routing over English, Hinglish and Hindi: the multilingual static encoder reached 87.0% overall (87.5%
Devanagari) at 0.03 ms; the small English static encoder fell to **45.3% on Devanagari**. A char n-gram TF-IDF
baseline is strong in English (90.9%) but drops on Hinglish (72.7%). **Used here:** Jev, policy retrieval and case
memory all use `potion-multilingual-128M`, CPU only.

## 7. Jev: ticket-only triage (this repo)

Jev decides from the ticket alone, before any system lookup. Training tickets: the clean ticket per benchmark case plus
variants with a required detail dropped, callers asking for a human, and callers whose words come from the attack
suite (3,186 tickets; `data/jev_tickets.jsonl`).

| split | route acc | route ECE | deployed missed human | deployed AI coverage | rules-only missed human | manipulation recall |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| test | 92.3% | 0.027 | **1.9%** | 70% | 11.4% | 92% |
| ood | 88.4% | 0.074 | 5.2% | 44% | 26.2% | 92% |
| adv | 83.7% | 0.082 | 2.3% | 61% | 7.0% | 24%* |

λ̂ = 0.883 at α = 2%. Temperature scaling cut route ECE from 0.068 to 0.027 in distribution. The model cuts missed
human tickets 6× versus the same rules without it.

\*Most adversarial content in that split sits in system-side CRM notes, which a ticket does not contain; the
caller-side attacks are caught by the text guard and policy triggers, which is why deployed missed-human is 2.3%.
Off-distribution the guarantee does not hold, hence the rules, the ceiling and feedback revocation.

## 8. Retrieval: selection, not recall, is the bottleneck

In our RAG work over large regulatory PDF collections (ALPHA), the hybrid candidate pool already held enough gold for
precision 0.95; most of the loss came from choosing which candidates to keep. Re-judging candidates from the pattern of
verdicts on their page lifted F1 from 0.154 (hybrid BM25 + dense top-100) to 0.569. **Used here:** the policy tree
(document → page → clause) with dense + BM25 fusion, neighbour borrowing and a hard document filter. Policy *holds* are
explicit triggers, not similarity, because retrieval tells you what a clause is about, not whether its condition holds.

## 9. Autonomy: revoke fast, earn slowly *(prior system → feedback.py)*

Our per-intent autonomy ladder granted autonomy with sequential tests and revoked it with CUSUM on outcomes. Here
the same asymmetry runs on support's verdicts: one bad AI outcome with no track record revokes AI for that scenario;
it comes back only after a run of verdicts showing AI would have been enough.

## Limitations

- The data is simulated, not real Paytm tickets. The method only needs (ticket, route, outcome) triples; real resolved
  tickets and support's verdicts replace the simulator.
- Off-distribution, Jev's coverage drops (44%) because it routes unfamiliar tickets to humans, which is the safe
  failure.
- Subtle multi-turn social engineering is not covered by the attack suite.
